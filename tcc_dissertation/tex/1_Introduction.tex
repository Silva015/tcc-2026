\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec_problem}

Firearms are among the instruments most used to commit homicides worldwide~\cite{unodc2023homicide}. Gunshot wounds are therefore common in forensic work, at crime scenes and in autopsies, and forensic experts must determine their nature. Two questions are central: whether a wound is an entry or an exit wound, and, for entry wounds, from which distance the shot was fired, the medico-legal shooting distance (MLSD).

Under ideal conditions, features such as the shape and size of the wound, the abrasion ring and the margins make entry and exit relatively easy to tell apart. In practice these features may be partly absent, overlapping or altered by putrefaction, and the calibre, the ammunition, the angle, intermediate targets and the body region complicate the classification even for experienced forensic pathologists.

Deep learning has changed medical image analysis, but its use in forensic practice is still limited. One of the main obstacles is data: forensic photographs are taken with different equipment, under varied conditions and in the routine of crime scenes, which makes standardisation impractical and produces small, imbalanced datasets. Lira et al.~\cite{Lira2025deep} built the first large database of real crime-scene photographs of gunshot wounds, the FDCPUnBGunshotDB, and evaluated 59 deep learning architectures on it.

This work started from a specific proposal: TransferKAN, a ResNet-152 pre-trained on ImageNet whose dense classification head is replaced by a Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN)~\cite{liu2024kan}. An earlier version of this dissertation reported that TransferKAN outperformed the baselines of Lira et al. Re-examining that comparison showed that it mixed different things. The published baselines came from a split in which, according to the public code of the study, images of the same case appear in training and test, with most architectures trained from scratch and the AUC computed from predicted classes. TransferKAN, in turn, had been evaluated with case-grouped folds, a pre-trained backbone, the AUC from probabilities and an epoch chosen on the test set. A difference between such numbers says little about the KAN head. This dissertation rebuilds the evaluation from scratch, under a single protocol, and asks what can actually be concluded.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec_goals}

The work is organised around five research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does the hold-out used in 2024 inflate the reported numbers, and by how much, when everything else is kept fixed?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} Under a protocol without case leakage, does a KAN head improve over linear and MLP heads on the same pre-trained backbone?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} How do twelve of the architectures evaluated by Lira et al.\ compare under the same protocol, and where does TransferKAN stand among them?
    \item \textbf{RQ4:} Does a KAN with a different basis, wavelets instead of splines, change the answer to RQ2?
    \item \textbf{RQ5:} Can a single network with one head per task replace the pair of networks currently used for entry/exit and shooting distance?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Original Hypothesis}
\label{sec_hypothesis}

The hypothesis that motivated TransferKAN was that replacing the MLP head of a pre-trained ResNet-152 with a KAN would improve discrimination, measured by the AUC, in both tasks, because learnable edge functions would model the decision boundaries of imbalanced forensic classes better. This hypothesis is not discarded; it becomes RQ2 and is tested with a controlled ablation and a decision rule fixed before the experiments (Section~\ref{sec:stats}). Its answer is negative.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec_contributions}

The contributions of this dissertation are methodological and practical:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{An audit of the 2024 split.} The hold-out of the public code of Lira et al.\ stratifies by case instead of grouping by case. Reproducing it shows that 98.7\% (entry/exit) and 98.4\% (shooting distance) of the test images have their case in training, and that, with the same network and recipe, this split inflates the AUC by $+0.034$ in entry/exit and by $+0.155$ to $+0.158$ in shooting distance.
    \item \textbf{A single protocol without leakage.} Case-grouped folds, one training recipe for every model, AUC from probabilities, a frozen and hashed implementation and decision rules written before the runs. Under it, twelve architectures, five heads, five wavelet-KAN heads and a multitask network were trained and compared.
    \item \textbf{A controlled head ablation with spline and wavelet KANs.} The KAN head of TransferKAN is equivalent to linear and MLP heads in entry/exit and below an MLP of the same size in shooting distance. Wavelet KANs do not change this, and neither family is parameter-efficient or faster in this setting. The best architecture is Swin-B, which outperforms TransferKAN in both tasks.
    \item \textbf{One network for both tasks at half the cost.} A single ResNet-152 with two heads has about half the parameters of the pair of networks and obtains both outputs twice as fast. It is non-inferior in shooting distance and slightly but consistently worse in entry/exit, within the pre-registered margin.
\end{itemize}
The results also show where the difficulty lies: contact shots, with 42 images from 16 cases, are almost never recognised when their case is absent from training.
