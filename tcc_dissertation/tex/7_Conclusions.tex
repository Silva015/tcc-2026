\label{chap:conclusions}

This dissertation set out to evaluate TransferKAN, a pre-trained ResNet-152 with a KAN classification head, for two forensic tasks on real crime-scene photographs: entry versus exit wounds and the medico-legal shooting distance. Re-examining an earlier comparison showed that its numbers could not be attributed to the KAN head. The work was therefore rebuilt around a single evaluation protocol without case leakage, with decision rules fixed before the experiments.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:respostas}

\textbf{RQ1: does the 2024 hold-out inflate the numbers?} Yes. The hold-out of the public code of Lira et al.\ stratifies by case instead of grouping by case, so 98.7\% (entry/exit) and 98.4\% (distance) of its test images belong to cases seen in training. With the same network, recipe and seed, it raises the AUC from 0.906 to 0.940 in entry/exit ($+0.034$) and from 0.779--0.788 to 0.937--0.942 in shooting distance ($+0.155$ to $+0.158$). The effect is large where the task is hard and the classes are rare, and it also hides the variability across cases. Numbers obtained with this split are not comparable with case-grouped results.

\textbf{RQ2: does the KAN head improve over linear and MLP heads?} No. On the same ResNet-152 and recipe, the KAN head is equivalent to the linear and MLP heads in entry/exit (AUC 0.905 against 0.904--0.906; difference to the best MLP $-0.001$, 95\% CI $[-0.002, +0.0003]$; outcome B). In shooting distance it is below the MLP with the same number of parameters ($-0.011$, $[-0.021, -0.003]$; outcome C) and equivalent to the MLP-128 and to the linear head. The KAN head of TransferKAN is not the source of its results, and with 3.94 million parameters it is not parameter-efficient.

\textbf{RQ3: how do the backbones compare?} With ImageNet pre-training and the same recipe, Swin-B is the best architecture in both tasks (AUC 0.929 in entry/exit and 0.838 in distance) and outperforms TransferKAN in both. TransferKAN sits in the middle of the twelve architectures, together with the ResNet-152 with a linear head. Compared with the published table of Lira et al., the other eleven backbones score higher than their published counterparts in entry/exit, while TransferKAN remains below their ResNet-152 in accuracy, F1 and AUC$_c$ in both tasks. These differences come from the split, the pre-training and the recipe together, and not from the KAN head.

\textbf{RQ4: does a wavelet KAN change the picture?} No. In entry/exit the wavelet-KAN heads rank the images as well as the MLPs (equal AUC) but lose about one point of accuracy and a little F1 at the default threshold. The contrast with an MLP of the same size is inconclusive. In shooting distance they are clearly worse (AUC about 0.71 against 0.80). They are not faster, and their learned scales and translations barely moved from initialisation.

\textbf{RQ5: can one network replace the pair?} Partly. One ResNet-152 with two heads has 47--50\% fewer parameters than the pair, trains in 8.1--8.6 instead of 14.6--14.9 minutes per fold and obtains both outputs twice as fast. It is non-inferior in shooting distance and worse in entry/exit by about 0.01 of AUC in every fold, which is within the pre-registered margin of 0.02 but consistent, with a larger drop in F1. When the cost matters more than one hundredth of AUC in entry/exit, it is a reasonable replacement.

Taken together, the hypothesis that motivated this work, that replacing the MLP head with a KAN improves the classification, is answered negatively and with two KAN families. The positive contributions are methodological, an audited split and a protocol under which the architectures can be compared, and practical, a single network for both tasks at about half the cost.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The answers above hold within limits set by the data, by the experimental design and by the comparison with earlier work. The first and most important limit comes from the data. All images come from a single police force in a single region, and no external validation was possible, so it is not known how the models would behave on photographs taken by other teams, with other equipment and under other conditions. Within this database, the contact class is the most restrictive case: it has only 42 images from 16 cases, and only 13--17\% of these images are recognised when their case is not in the training set (Section~\ref{sec:results_mlsd}). With so few cases, nothing specific can be claimed about this class.

The experimental design imposes further restrictions. Every model is evaluated at the last epoch, without an inner validation split. Because the rule is the same for all models, it is fair across them, but it is not tuned for any of them. Likewise, the recipe of the original TransferKAN notebook was kept for every model, including a strong Gaussian noise that may erase fine traces such as stippling; it does not favour any model, but other recipes might change the absolute numbers. The number of seeds also varies between experiments: Swin-B and MaxViT-T have two seeds in the distance task, and most other backbones, the small KAN (e) and the wavelet heads W3--W5 have a single seed. The results of these models therefore carry more uncertainty than those of the head ablation, which uses three seeds (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

Finally, two limitations concern the comparison with Lira et al. The leakage experiment reproduces the method of their public code, not the exact partition behind their published tables, which cannot be recovered; it measures the effect of that splitting method, and the code used for the article may have differed. In addition, the comparison with the published table suggests a large effect of ImageNet pre-training, but no model was trained from scratch under the single protocol, so that effect is suggested by this work but not measured in it (Section~\ref{sec:res_lira}). Most of these limitations apply equally to every model and therefore do not bias the comparisons made under the single protocol; what they restrict is how far the absolute numbers and the conclusions extend beyond this database.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

The results and the limitations above suggest five directions for future work. The first follows directly from the head ablation. Since the KAN head did not improve the ResNet-152, it remains to be seen whether this result holds on a stronger feature extractor, which can be tested by training a KAN head on Swin-B, the best backbone under the single protocol.

The second direction addresses the main limitation of the data. Collecting more contact cases, as well as images from other institutions, would allow the minority class to be learned and the models to be validated outside the original database, so that the conclusions would no longer depend on a single police force and region.

The third and fourth directions concern the alternatives evaluated in this work. The wavelet heads and the multitask network lose mainly at the default threshold (Sections~\ref{sec:res_wavkan} and~\ref{sec:res_mt}), which motivates calibrating the probabilities and choosing the decision threshold on an inner validation split. For the multitask network in particular, weighting the task losses, for example by their uncertainty, could recover its small loss in entry/exit and strengthen its case as a replacement for the pair of networks.

The last direction concerns the factor that this work could not isolate. The differences from the published numbers of Lira et al.\ were attributed to the split, the pre-training and the recipe, but only the effect of the split was measured. Training some backbones from scratch under the single protocol would measure the effect of pre-training as well, and would make the comparison with the earlier results more complete.
