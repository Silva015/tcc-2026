\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter describes the single evaluation protocol under which every model of this work was trained and tested. All models share the same images, the same five case-grouped folds, the same training recipe, the same metrics and the same statistical procedure; only the component under study (the head, the backbone, the split or the number of tasks) changes from one experiment to another. The protocol was written as one Python module (\texttt{protocolo\_comum.py}, version 1.0.0, SHA-256 \texttt{b7cd9bac\ldots}) and frozen before the first training run. The later experiments (shooting distance, the reproduction of the split of Lira et al., the wavelet-KAN heads and the multitask network) were added as plug-ins that register new heads, configurations or losses without editing the module, and every run records the hashes of the module, of the plug-in and of the fold file, so that it can be checked that all numbers come from the same code.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset_desc}

The images come from the FDCPUnBGunshotDB~\cite{Lira2025deep}, a collection of photographs of gunshot wounds taken at crime scenes and autopsies by the Federal District Civil Police (PCDF), Brazil. The database has no redistribution licence and its README requires citing Lira et al.~\cite{Lira2025deep}; it was cloned at a fixed commit (\texttt{f679389}) in every session~\cite{fdcpunbgunshotdb}, and no image or image cache left the training sessions.

Labels are read from the folder structure. The snapshot used contains 2,554 images from 486 cases: 1,883 entry wounds and 671 exit wounds. The 1,883 entries are further labelled with the medico-legal shooting distance (MLSD): 1,609 distant, 232 close-range and 42 contact shots; the 42 contact images come from only 16 cases. Lira et al.\ describe 2,551 images (1,883 entries and 668 exits) from 489 cases~\cite{Lira2025deep}. The difference in images comes from three exit images that are present in the folders but absent from the CSV distributed with the database; the CSV lists 2,551 images from 485 cases. The 489 cases of the article are not reproduced by the distributed data. In addition, cases are identified here by year and case number (the first nine characters of the file name), whereas the code of Lira et al.\ uses the case number without the year, which merges 56 numbers that occur in more than one year (417 groups instead of 486)~\cite{leguwoi}. One exit image is labelled with a distance in the CSV; the folder label (exit) is used. The age distribution of the victims is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:age_dist}.

\section{Case-Grouped Folds}
\label{sec:folds}

Photographs of the same case (often several views of the same wound, or of wounds of the same victim) are strongly correlated. If images of one case fall in training and test, the test set no longer measures generalisation to new cases (Section~\ref{sec:leakage_background}). All experiments therefore use one set of five folds in which every case belongs to exactly one fold. The folds were generated once with \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} from scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit} (five folds, shuffled, seed 42) over the image list sorted by path, with groups given by the case and stratification by a four-class label (exit, contact, close, distant), so that both tasks are balanced at the same time. A composition rule was fixed before the folds were generated: every test fold must contain at least two contact cases and at least one image of each class, and if seed 42 failed, the first seed $\geq 43$ that passed would be used. Seed 42 passed. Table~\ref{tab:dobras} shows the result; the share of exits per fold stays between 24.5\% and 28.9\% (26.3\% overall).

The distance task uses the same folds restricted to the entries, so no case crosses folds in either task, and the two tasks can be compared and combined image by image.

\section{Training Recipe}
\label{sec:recipe}

Every model (heads, backbones, wavelet-KAN heads and multitask network) is trained with the recipe of the original TransferKAN notebook, which is now applied to all of them:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Model:} a torchvision~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} backbone with its default ImageNet weights~\cite{deng2009imagenet}, followed by flattening, Dropout ($p=0.5$) and the head.
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 (epochs 1--15):} backbone frozen (batch-normalisation statistics still updated, as in the original code); only the new parameters are optimised with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$) and cosine annealing~\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr} over 15 epochs.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 (epochs 16--60):} the whole network is trained with AdamW (learning rate $10^{-5}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$) and cosine annealing over 45 epochs.
    \item \textbf{Batches and loss:} batches of 32 images, dropping the last incomplete batch; cross-entropy with class weights $n/(K\,n_c)$ computed on the training part of each fold ($K$ classes, $n_c$ images of class $c$).
    \item \textbf{No internal validation and no epoch selection:} the whole training part of the fold is used for training, and the model evaluated is the one at epoch 60. The test fold is evaluated once.
    \item \textbf{Augmentation} (training only, Albumentations~\cite{buslaev2020albumentations} 2.0.8, with the values that actually ran in the original notebook): resize to $224 \times 224$; horizontal and vertical flips ($p=0.5$ each); shift-scale-rotate (shift 0.05, scale 0.1, rotation up to $30^\circ$, $p=0.5$); CLAHE (clip 2.0, $8 \times 8$ tiles, $p=0.3$); elastic transform ($\alpha=1$, $\sigma=50$, $p=0.3$); Gaussian noise (standard deviation between 0.2 and 0.44 of the intensity range, $p=0.2$); colour jitter (brightness, contrast and saturation 0.2, hue 0.05, $p=0.5$); ImageNet normalisation. Test images are only resized and normalised.
    \item \textbf{Seeds:} the folds are fixed; the training seed (42, 43 or 44) controls the head initialisation, the dropout masks, the batch order and the augmentation.
\end{itemize}

The original notebook was written for an older Albumentations interface; two of its arguments (\texttt{alpha\_affine} and \texttt{var\_limit}) are silently discarded by version 2, so the elastic transform and the Gaussian noise ran with the values listed above rather than with the values written in the notebook. The protocol fixes those effective values explicitly, so the augmentation is identical to the one that produced the original results and is the same for every model.

\textbf{Numerical precision and hardware.} All comparable runs used an NVIDIA L4 GPU on Google Colab. The backbone runs in automatic mixed precision (fp16)~\cite{micikevicius2018mixed} and the heads always in fp32. Mixed precision was adopted only after a check defined before any run: the TransferKAN was trained on fold 1 with seed 42 in fp32 and in mixed precision, and mixed precision would be accepted if accuracy changed by at most 2 percentage points and AUC by at most 0.015. Accuracy was 84.98\% in fp32 and 84.39\% in mixed precision, and AUC 0.9078 and 0.9076, while the time per fold fell from 14.9 to 8.1 minutes; mixed precision was therefore used for every model.

\section{Heads Compared on the ResNet-152}
\label{sec:heads_setup}

The head ablation keeps the ResNet-152 backbone (58,143,808 parameters), the folds and the recipe fixed and changes only the layer that maps the 2,048 features to the classes. The five heads, with their parameter counts, are listed in Table~\ref{tab:cabecas} (Chapter~\ref{chap:solution}):
(a) a linear layer; (b) an MLP with 128 hidden units; (c) an MLP whose hidden width (1,919) gives it practically the same number of parameters as the KAN (3.94 million); (d) the KAN head of TransferKAN, $[2048, 128, K]$ with grid size 10 and cubic splines, implemented with \texttt{efficient-kan}~\cite{efficientkan} (15 parameters per edge: 13 B-spline coefficients, one base weight and one spline scale); and (e) a small KAN, $[2048, 8, 2]$, with roughly the budget of (b). Heads (a)--(d) were trained with seeds 42, 43 and 44 in both tasks; head (e) only with seed 42 and only for entry/exit.

\section{Backbones Under the Same Protocol}
\label{sec:backbones_setup}

To place TransferKAN among the architectures evaluated by Lira et al., twelve of the 59 architectures of their Table~4 were retrained with the same recipe. They cover convolutional networks from 0.3 to 134 million parameters and two transformer-based architectures, and include their best model, the ResNet-152~\cite{he2016resnet} with a linear head, which is head (a). The other eleven are AlexNet~\cite{krizhevsky2012alexnet}, MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}, MNASNet 1.3~\cite{tan2019mnasnet}, ShuffleNetV2 $\times$0.5~\cite{ma2018shufflenetv2}, SqueezeNet 1.0~\cite{iandola2016squeezenet}, DenseNet-169~\cite{huang2017densenet}, MaxViT-T~\cite{tu2022maxvit}, Swin-B~\cite{liu2021swin}, EfficientNetV2-L~\cite{tan2021efficientnetv2}, VGG-16~\cite{simonyan2015vgg} and RegNetY-16GF~\cite{radosavovic2020regnet}. The catalogue follows the code of Lira et al.~\cite{leguwoi}: the same torchvision constructors and the same point where the last layer is replaced. Two deviations are declared: every backbone starts from the default ImageNet weights (in the public code of Lira et al.\ only the ResNets are pre-trained, and their first convolution is replaced by a randomly initialised one), and the first convolution is kept. The new last layer is Dropout(0.5) followed by a linear layer (a $1 \times 1$ convolution in the SqueezeNet). EfficientNetV2-L, pre-trained at 480 px, runs at 224 px like the others. Swin-B and MaxViT-T were trained with seeds 42, 43 and 44 for entry/exit and with seeds 42 and 43 for the shooting distance; the other backbones with seed 42 only.

\section{Shooting Distance}
\label{sec:mlsd_setup}

The distance task (contact, close range, distant) uses only entry wounds, since exits carry no distance information. For test fold $k$, the network is trained on the entries of the other four folds and evaluated on the entries of fold $k$. The heads have $K=3$ outputs, and everything else (recipe, seeds, grid size 10 of the KAN) is as in the entry/exit task. The earlier distance model of this work, trained in a single phase with a grid of 5 and with the epoch chosen on the test set, was discarded and is not used anywhere in this dissertation.

\section{Reproducing the Split of Lira et al.}
\label{sec:leak_setup}

To measure how much the split alone changes the results, the hold-out of Lira et al.\ was reproduced from their public code (\texttt{iterative\_split} in \texttt{leguwoi}, commit \texttt{83d82f1})~\cite{leguwoi}. That function splits images 80/20 with iterative stratification~\cite{sechidis2011stratification} (\texttt{IterativeStratification} of scikit-multilearn 0.2.0, order 2) on two columns, the case number and the label. Stratifying on the case number spreads the images of each case over training and test, instead of keeping them together. The function fixes no seed, so three partitions were generated with seeds 42, 43 and 44, on the 2,551 images of the distributed CSV (entry/exit: 2,074 training and 477 test images; distance: 1,520 and 363 entries). The ResNet-152 with heads (a) and (d) was trained on each partition with the recipe above (seed 42) and compared with the same models on the case-grouped folds (seed 42). This reproduces the method of the public code, not necessarily the exact partition used for the published tables, which cannot be recovered.

\section{Wavelet-KAN Heads}
\label{sec:wavkan_setup}

To test whether a different family of KAN changes the conclusion of the head ablation, wavelet-KAN (Wav-KAN) heads~\cite{bozorgasl2024wavkan} were added. Each layer follows the \texttt{WavKANLayer} of \texttt{torch-conv-kan}~\cite{drokin2024torchconvkan}: every edge has a learnable scale, a learnable translation, a wavelet weight and a weight for a SiLU base branch, and the layer output goes through batch normalisation. The implementation used here was checked against \texttt{torch-conv-kan} (commit \texttt{b412a3b}) and gave identical outputs and gradients on CPU and GPU. The heads have two layers, $[2048, h, K]$, and $8202h + 4$ parameters for $K=2$. Five configurations were trained (Table~\ref{tab:cabecas}): W1, Mexican hat with $h=128$ (the topology of head (d), with 3.7 times fewer parameters); W2, Mexican hat with $h=32$ (the budget of the MLP (b)); W3 and W4, derivative-of-Gaussian and Morlet wavelets with $h=128$ (sensitivity to the wavelet); and W5, Mexican hat with $h=480$ (the budget of (d)). The Mexican hat was fixed in advance for the matched budgets. W1 and W2 were trained with seeds 42--44 in both tasks; W3--W5 only with seed 42 and only for entry/exit. Besides the time per fold, the head alone was benchmarked on random 2048-dimensional inputs (forward and backward pass for a batch of 32 and inference per image; 20 warm-up and 200 timed runs with CUDA events).

\section{One Network for Both Tasks}
\label{sec:mt_setup}

The two tasks are currently solved by two separate networks. The multitask experiment trains one ResNet-152 with a shared backbone and Dropout and two heads, one for entry/exit ($K=2$) and one for distance ($K=3$)~\cite{caruana1997multitask,ruder2017overview}. Both heads are of the same type, either the KAN (d) or the MLP-128 (b). The loss is $L = L_{\mathrm{E/S}} + L_{\mathrm{dist}}$: each term is a class-weighted cross-entropy, and exits are masked out of the distance term (their distance label is undefined), so the entry/exit head learns from all 2,554 images and the distance head from the 1,883 entries. When a batch contains no entry, the distance term is set to zero. The single-task references are the networks of the head ablation, (d) and (b), for each task, trained with the same seeds (42--44); the comparison is therefore paired on the same test images.

Two further measures are reported. A four-class pipeline (exit, contact, close, distant) combines the two outputs: with the single-task pair, the entry/exit network decides and, for predicted entries, the distance network decides; with the multitask network, its two heads do the same. This measure is secondary and descriptive. The cost is reported as parameters, training time per fold and the time to obtain both outputs (one forward pass of the multitask network against two forward passes of the pair), measured on the L4 in mixed precision with batches of 1 and 32 (20 warm-up and 200 timed runs). Inference time does not depend on the weights and was measured on freshly built models; no trained weights were stored.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

For each fold and each seed, the following are computed on the test images: accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro precision, recall and F1, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC)~\cite{fawcett2006roc} computed from the predicted probabilities. In the binary task the AUC uses the probability of the exit class; in the distance task it is the macro average of the three one-vs-rest AUCs, computed on entries only. Results are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation across the five folds, where each fold is first averaged over the seeds; the pooled out-of-fold AUC (AUC$_{\mathrm{OOF}}$), computed after averaging each image's probabilities over the seeds, is also reported. In the distance task accuracy is misleading: always predicting ``distant'' already gives 0.854. The main metrics are therefore AUC and macro F1.

Lira et al.\ computed their AUC from predicted classes rather than probabilities~\cite{leguwoi}. For a single threshold the ROC curve has one point, and the macro AUC reduces to the average of recall and specificity; this quantity, called AUC$_c$ here, is computed only to compare with their tables (Section~\ref{sec:auc_background}).

\section{Statistics and Pre-Registered Decision Rules}
\label{sec:stats}

Two models are always compared on the same test images. The difference $\Delta$ (first minus second) is computed on the pooled out-of-fold predictions, and its 95\% confidence interval comes from a paired bootstrap over cases~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}: cases are resampled with replacement 2,000 times, which respects the dependence between images of the same case. The difference per fold and the number of folds in which each model wins are also reported.

The decision rules were written before the corresponding runs. The rules of the head ablation, the backbones and the distance task were fixed in the experiment plans and in the analysis notebooks committed to the repository on 14 October 2026 (commits \texttt{9961146} and \texttt{367a7a6}, which remain in its history), before the first training run:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary comparison:} the KAN head (d) against the better of the two MLPs, (b) or (c), in AUC.
    \item \textbf{Outcome A (the KAN improves):} $\Delta$AUC $> 0$ in at least four of the five folds \emph{and} a confidence interval above zero.
    \item \textbf{Outcome C (the KAN is worse):} the KAN wins at most one fold \emph{and} the interval is below zero.
    \item \textbf{Outcome B (equivalent):} otherwise.
    \item \textbf{Claims forbidden without the corresponding result:} ``only changing the head improves'', ``new state of the art'', ``outperforms the transformer baselines'' and ``parameter efficiency''.
\end{itemize}

The wavelet-KAN and multitask experiments were planned in the same commit of plans (\texttt{9961146}), with a non-inferiority margin of $-0.02$: a model is \emph{non-inferior} when the lower bound of the interval of $\Delta$AUC (and, for the wavelet-KAN heads, also of $\Delta$F1) is above $-0.02$. Superior and inferior follow the rule of outcomes A and C. For the wavelet-KAN heads, the phrase ``more parameter-efficient than an MLP'' was allowed only if W2 were superior to the MLP (b) of the same size, and ``faster'' only if both the head benchmark and the time per fold improved beyond run-to-run noise. On 19 October 2026, after the first experiment and before any wavelet-KAN or multitask run, three adjustments were recorded (commit \texttt{3266a99}): the main configurations would use seeds 42--44, as the references; W1 and W2 would also be run on the distance task; and the multitask comparison would reuse the single-task networks of the head ablation. The margin was kept.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{No validation split.} Evaluating the last epoch avoids selecting the epoch on the test set, which inflated the earlier results of this work, but it is not tuned either: a model stopped by an inner validation split could be slightly better. The rule is the same for every model, so it does not favour any of them.

\textbf{Augmentation as it ran.} The Gaussian noise of the original recipe is strong (standard deviation up to 0.44 of the intensity range) and may erase fine traces such as stippling, which matter for the close-range class. It applies equally to all models; it is a limitation of the recipe, not a bias of the comparisons.

\textbf{Seeds.} The head ablation, Swin-B and MaxViT-T (entry/exit), W1, W2 and the multitask networks use three seeds. The other backbones, head (e) and W3--W5 use one seed, and Swin-B and MaxViT-T use two seeds in the distance task; these results are reported with that caveat.

\textbf{The split of Lira et al.} The leakage experiment reproduces the method of the public code with three seeds; the exact partition behind the published tables cannot be recovered, and the code may differ from the one used for the article.

\textbf{Statistical power and the contact class.} Five folds give little power to detect small differences between heads. The contact class has 42 images from 16 cases (4 to 13 test images per fold), so nothing specific is claimed about it.

\textbf{A single database.} All images come from one police force and one region; no external validation was possible.
